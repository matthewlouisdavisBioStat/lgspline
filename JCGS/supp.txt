\section*{S.A Multivariate Basis Example and Constraint Counting}\label{sec:supp_counting}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{S.A Multivariate Basis Example}

The main paper (Section~2.1) illustrates the monomial basis in the
single-predictor cubic case. Here we give a multivariate example omitted
there for brevity.

Consider $q=3$ predictors $t, u, v \in \mathbb{R}$. For each observation
$i$, a $p$-dimensional vector of up-to-quartic polynomial basis expansions
with linear, quadratic, cubic, and quartic main effects, linear
interactions, linear-quadratic interactions, and a single three-way linear
interaction, can be written as follows. The same construction extends
directly to more than three predictors, although we restrict attention
here to at most three-way interactions.

Let
\[
\bm{x}_i = (1,\,
t_i, t_i^2, t_i^3, t_i^4,\,
u_i, u_i^2, u_i^3, u_i^4,\,
v_i, v_i^2, v_i^3, v_i^4,\,
t_i u_i,\,
t_i v_i,\,
u_i v_i,\,
t_i u_i^2,\,
u_i t_i^2,\,
t_i v_i^2,\,
v_i t_i^2,\,
u_i v_i^2,\,
v_i u_i^2,\,
t_i u_i v_i)^{\T}.
\]
Because the expansion in each predictor, conditional on the others, is
in monomial form, taking first derivatives with respect to any predictor
is straightforward. For example,
\[
  \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bm{x}_i =
  (0,\,
  1, 2t_i, 3t_i^2, 4t_i^3,\,
  0, 0, 0, 0,\,
  0, 0, 0, 0,\,
  u_i,\,
  v_i,\,
  0,\,
  u_i^2,\,
  2u_i t_i,\,
  v_i^2,\,
  2v_i t_i,\,
  0,\,
  0,\,
  u_i v_i)^{\T}.
\]
The three partial derivatives can be combined as
\[
\bm{x}'_i =
\begin{pmatrix}
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bm{x}_i \\
\frac{\partial}{\partial u}\bm{x}_i \\
\frac{\partial}{\partial v}\bm{x}_i
\end{pmatrix},
\]
to construct a $3 \times p$ matrix.

Similarly,
\[
\bm{x}''_i =
\begin{pmatrix}
\frac{\partial^2}{\partial t^2}\bm{x}_i + \frac{\partial^2}{\partial t \partial u}\bm{x}_i + \frac{\partial^2}{\partial t \partial v}\bm{x}_i \\
\frac{\partial^2}{\partial u^2}\bm{x}_i + \frac{\partial^2}{\partial t \partial u}\bm{x}_i + \frac{\partial^2}{\partial u \partial v}\bm{x}_i \\
\frac{\partial^2}{\partial v^2}\bm{x}_i + \frac{\partial^2}{\partial t \partial v}\bm{x}_i + \frac{\partial^2}{\partial u \partial v}\bm{x}_i
\end{pmatrix}
\]
is a $3 \times p$ matrix of predictor-specific ``total'' second
derivatives, matching the convention used by \pkg{lgspline}: for each
predictor, the pure second derivative and the mixed second derivatives
involving that predictor are combined into one row. The smoothing
constraints imply that at each knot separating partitions $\mathcal{P}_k$
and $\mathcal{P}_{k^{*}}$ with expansions evaluated at the shared
boundary point:

\[
\bm{x}_{k,k^{*}}^{\T}(\bbeta_k - \bbeta_{k^{*}}) =
\bm{x}_{k,k^{*}}^{'\T}(\bbeta_k - \bbeta_{k^{*}}) =
\bm{x}_{k,k^{*}}^{''\T}(\bbeta_k - \bbeta_{k^{*}}) = 0.
\]
The constraints can be encoded in a matrix
$\bA \in \mathbb{R}^{P \times R}$ such that the smoothing constraints are
met when $\bA^{\T}\bbeta = \bm{0}$. Additional linear constraints can
also be included, or specific smoothness constraints removed, by adding
or deleting columns from the constraint matrix. For illustration, let
$K=3$ knots define 4 partitions separated by three knot locations
$\bm{x}_{1,2}, \bm{x}_{2,3}, \bm{x}_{3,4}$:

\begingroup
\small
\[
\bA^{\T}\bbeta = \begin{pmatrix}
\bm{x}^{\T}_{1,2} & -\bm{x}^{\T}_{1,2} & \bm{0} & \bm{0} \\
\bm{x}'^{\T}_{1,2} & -\bm{x}'^{\T}_{1,2} & \bm{0} & \bm{0} \\
\bm{x}''^{\T}_{1,2} & - \bm{x}''^{\T}_{1,2} & \bm{0} & \bm{0} \\
\bm{0} & \bm{x}^{\T}_{2,3} & -\bm{x}^{\T}_{2,3} & \bm{0} \\
\bm{0} & \bm{x}'^{\T}_{2,3} & -\bm{x}'^{\T}_{2,3} & \bm{0} \\
\bm{0} & \bm{x}''^{\T}_{2,3} & - \bm{x}''^{\T}_{2,3} & \bm{0} \\
\bm{0} & \bm{0} & \bm{x}^{\T}_{3,4} & -\bm{x}^{\T}_{3,4} \\
\bm{0} & \bm{0} & \bm{x}'^{\T}_{3,4} & -\bm{x}'^{\T}_{3,4} \\
\bm{0} & \bm{0} & \bm{x}''^{\T}_{3,4} & - \bm{x}''^{\T}_{3,4}
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\bbeta_1 \\ \bbeta_2 \\ \bbeta_3 \\ \bbeta_4 \end{pmatrix}.
\]
\endgroup


\subsection*{S.A.2\quad Constraint counting: $R < P$}

The main paper states that $\bA$ is typically underdetermined in the
settings of primary interest. Here we give a simple bound that makes
this precise in the cubic case with up to second-derivative continuity
constraints.

Let $\phi$ denote the average number of knots per partition. For $q$
spline predictors and cubic bases with fitted-value, first-derivative,
and second-derivative matching at each knot, the constraint count is
\[
  R = \phi K(2q + 1),
\]
using the predictor-specific first- and second-derivative matching
convention from S.A above (one fitted-value constraint plus $2q$
derivative constraints per knot). The coefficient dimension satisfies
\[
  P = (K+1)p, \qquad p \geq 3q + 1,
\]
giving $P \geq (K+1)(3q+1)$. The number of expansions $3q+1$ refers to
linear, quadratic, and cubic terms per predictor plus an intercept. The
system is underdetermined, $R < P$, whenever
\[
  \phi K(2q+1) < (K+1)(3q+1)
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  \phi < \left(\frac{K+1}{K}\right)\left(\frac{3q+1}{2q+1}\right).
\]
A sufficient condition that removes the dependence on $K$ is
\[
  \phi \leq \frac{3q+1}{2q+1},
\]
since $(K+1)/K > 1$ for all $K \geq 1$. For $q = 1$ this gives
$\phi \leq 4/3$, and a single-predictor spline with exactly $K$ knots has
$\phi = 1$, so $R < P$ always holds in the single-predictor cubic
setting. More generally, if smoothness is enforced up to the $d$th
derivative instead of the second, the general condition is
$\phi \leq p/(dq+1)$.

In practice, higher-dimensional partitions can have many neighbors, so
$\phi$ grows with $q$ and the bound can fail. This is one reason the
method allows selected derivative constraints to be removed. Retaining
only fitted-value continuity reduces the per-knot constraint count and
makes $R < P$ easy to maintain in most settings of interest. When
interaction terms are added to the basis, $p$ grows while $R$ is
unchanged, since interactions do not introduce additional knots or
derivative matching conditions, so $R < P$ is only further loosened.


\section*{S.B\quad Woodbury Factorization for Structured Correlation}%
\label{sec:supp_woodbury}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{S.B Woodbury Factorization for Structured Correlation}

This supplement derives the structured-correlation acceleration
described in the main paper (Section~3.3). The goal is to preserve the
transformed-OLS projection while avoiding a dense $P \times P$
factorization, ans preserving as many opportunities for parallelism as possible.

Write
\[
  \bG^{-1} = \bX^\T\bV^{-1}\bX + \bm{\Lambda}
           = \bG^{-1}_{\mathrm{on}} + \bG^{-1}_{\mathrm{off}},
\]
where $\bG^{-1}_{\mathrm{on}}$ contains the $K+1$ block-diagonal
$p \times p$ pieces and $\bG^{-1}_{\mathrm{off}}$ contains the
remaining cross-partition terms. Without structured correlation,
$\bG^{-1}_{\mathrm{off}} = \bm{0}$. The Woodbury route is useful when
$\bG^{-1}_{\mathrm{off}}$ is sparse and low rank relative to the full
coefficient dimension, which is common when many partitions are present
but each correlated subject trajectory spans only a few of them.

Let $\bm{E}\bm{J}\bm{E}^{\T}$ denote a reduced factorization of
$\bG^{-1}_{\mathrm{off}}$ retaining only the $s$ nonzero singular
directions. The Woodbury identity gives
\begin{align}
    \bG = \bG_{\mathrm{on}} - \bG_{\mathrm{on}}\bm{E}(\bm{J}^{-1}+\bm{E}^{\T}\bG_{\mathrm{on}}\bm{E})^{-1}\bm{E}^{\T}\bG_{\mathrm{on}}.
      \\ = \bG_{\mathrm{on}}^{1/2}
        \underbrace{\Bigl[\bI - \bG_{\mathrm{on}}^{1/2}\bm{E}
        (\bm{J}^{-1}+\bm{E}^{\T}\bG_{\mathrm{on}}\bm{E})^{-1}
        \bm{E}^{\T}\bG_{\mathrm{on}}^{1/2}\Bigr]}_{\displaystyle \bF}
        \bG^{1/2}_{\mathrm{on}}.
\end{align}
Define $\bm{N} = \bG^{1/2}_{\mathrm{on}}\bm{E}$. Then
\begin{align}
    \bF = \bI - \bm{N}(\bm{J}^{-1}+\bm{N}^{\T}\bm{N})^{-1}\bm{N}^{\T}.
\end{align}
Applying Woodbury once more yields
\begin{align}
    \bF = (\bI + \bm{N}\bm{J}\bm{N}^{\T})^{-1}.
\end{align}
Substituting the QR decomposition $\bm{N} = \bQ\bR$, with
$\bm{P} = \bQ\bQ^\T$, gives the inverse of $\bF$ as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:INJN_decomp}
  \bF^{-1} = \bI + \bm{N}\bm{J}\bm{N}^\T
  = (\bI - \bm{P}) + \bm{P} + \bQ\bR\bm{J}\bR^\T\bQ^\T
  = (\bI - \bm{P}) + \bQ\underbrace{(\bI_s + \bR\bm{J}\bR^\T)}_{\displaystyle\bS}\bQ^\T.
\end{equation}
Because $\bI-\bm{P}$ and $\bQ\bS\bQ^\T$ act on orthogonal subspaces,
the square roots follow immediately:
\[
  \bF^{-1/2} = (\bI-\bm{P})+\bQ\bS^{1/2}\bQ^{\T},
  \qquad
  \bF^{1/2} = (\bI-\bm{P})+\bQ\bS^{-1/2}\bQ^{\T}.
\]
Therefore
\begin{align}
  \bG^{1/2}    &= \bG_{\mathrm{on}}^{1/2}\,\bF^{1/2}
                = \bG_{\mathrm{on}}^{1/2}
                  \bigl[(\bI - \bm{P}) + \bQ\bS^{-1/2}\bQ^\T\bigr],
                  \label{eq:G_half}\\[4pt]
  \bG^{-1/2}   &= \bF^{-1/2}\,\bG_{\mathrm{on}}^{-1/2}
                = \bigl[(\bI - \bm{P}) + \bQ\bS^{1/2}\bQ^\T\bigr]
                  \bG_{\mathrm{on}}^{-1/2}.
                  \label{eq:G_invhalf}
\end{align}
The non-block-diagonal part enters only through the projector $\bm{P}$
and the $s \times s$ matrix $\bS$.

The unconstrained GLS estimator is
$\bhbeta = \bG\bX^\T\bV^{-1}\by$.
The constrained estimator is obtained by replacing $\bG$ in the
projection formula from the main paper (Section~2.2):
\[
  \btbeta
  = \bigl[\bI - \bG\bA(\bA^\T\bG\bA)^{-1}\bA^\T\bigr]\bhbeta.
\]
As in the independent case, define the transformed quantities
\[
  \bw = \bX^\T\bV^{-1}\by,
  \qquad
  \by^* = \bG^{1/2}\bw,
  \qquad
  \bX^* = \bG^{1/2}\bA.
\]
Then
\[
  \br^*
  = \bigl[\bI - \bX^*(\bX^{*\T}\bX^*)^{-1}\bX^{*\T}\bigr]\by^*,
  \qquad
  \btbeta = \bG^{1/2}\br^*.
\]
Using~\eqref{eq:G_half}, each transformed quantity is a
block-diagonal multiplication through $\bG_{\mathrm{on}}^{1/2}$ plus a
rank-$s$ correction via $\bF^{1/2}$:
\begin{align*}
  \by^*
  &= \bG_{\mathrm{on}}^{1/2}
     \bF^{1/2}\bw,\\
  \bX^*
  &= \bG_{\mathrm{on}}^{1/2}
     \bF^{1/2}\bA,\\
  \btbeta
  &= \bG_{\mathrm{on}}^{1/2}
     \bF^{1/2}\br^*.
\end{align*}
The only QR decomposition on the full coefficient space is still that
of the $P \times R$ matrix $\bX^*$. If the constraints are
nonzero, $\bA^\T\bbeta = \bm{c}$, the same particular-solution
adjustment used in the main paper (Section~2.2) is added after
projection.

\section*{S.C\quad Penalty Tuning}\label{sec:supp_penalties}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{S.D Penalty Construction and Tuning}


Penalty parameters are estimated on the log scale
($\lambda = \exp(\theta)$). In \pkg{lgspline}, the default criterion is RMSE computed on the
exact leave-one-out cross-validation residuals under constraint
\citep{tarpey2000}. The leave-one-out values $y^{(-)}$ may be expressed
as:
\begin{align}
    y^{(-)}_i = y_i - \frac{y_i-\tilde{y}_i}{1-\bm{H}_{ii}},
\end{align}
where $\bm{H}_{ii}$ is the $i$th diagonal element of the hat matrix
$\bm{H}$. In the present setting, the constrained hat matrix is
\[
\bH = \bX \bU \bG \bX^\T,
\]
so the usual leverage adjustment is applied using the diagonal entries
of $\bH$.

An optional generalized cross-validation (GCV) \citep{golub1979} approximation
is also available:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gcv}
  \mathrm{GCV}_u = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N r_i^2}{N(1 - \bar{W})^2},
\end{equation}
where $r_i$ are residuals on the link scale, $\bar{W} = \tr(\bH)/N$,
and $\bH$ is the constrained hat matrix above. This is the direct
analogue of the usual GCV score, with the ordinary hat matrix replaced
by $\bX\bU\bG\bX^\T$. Closed-form gradients with respect to the
log-penalty parameters are available through Liebniz chain rule. The
implementation in \pkg{lgspline} uses a grid search to initialize a quasi-Newton
optimizer, and by default, the optimized penalties are divided by $((N+1)/(N-1))^2$
as a small-sample adjustment. 

To obtain residuals on the link scale for non-identity link function
$g$, consider a small non-negative scalar $\delta > 0$ and compute
$g(\frac{y+\delta}{1+2\delta})$ and
$g(\frac{\tilde{y}+\delta}{1+2\delta})$ to obtain predictions, avoiding computational issues with link functions such as logit
transforms of binary response or natural log transforms of counts of 0.
The implementation in \pkg{lgspline} automatically tunes $\delta$ by
default such that the marginal distribution of transformed $y$ follows
a $t$ distribution with $N-1$ degrees of freedom, as measured using the
MAE between observed and expected quantiles of the transformed response.
This is a computationally convenient approximation as it allows most of
the derivative machinery used for identity link to remain intact for
gradient-based optimization of penalty parameter through non-Gaussian and non-identity link settings. 

As mentioned in the main text, the derivative of $\lambda_w$ is computed first which allows for derivatives of all other penalties $\lambda_{l=1...L,k=1...K+1}, \lambda_r$ to computed efficiently. This relies on a heuristic presented below, which we hope to extend as standalone future work. 

For simplicity, suppose $\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_k = \lambda_w(\bLam_s + \lambda_r\bI + \lambda_{z}\bLam_{z})$ for all partitions, and suppose we differentiated the tuning criterion (either leave-one-out cross-validated RMSE or GCV under consraint) for $\lambda_w$, which in general involves many matrix and vector operations, even with block-diagonal structure leveraged. To quickly obtain derivatives for other penalties without repeating these operations, consider the following heuristic, abusing calculus notation for Leibniz chain rule (as this maps to a matrix rather than a scalar) for criterion $\ell$:

\begin{align}
    \frac{\partial \ell }{\lambda_{z}}= \sum^{K+1}_{k=1}\frac{\partial \bLam_k}{\lambda_{z}}\frac{\partial{\lambda_w}}{\partial \bLam_k}\frac{\partial{\ell}}{\lambda_w}.
\end{align}
By inverse derivative rule:
\begin{align}
    = \sum^{K+1}_{k=1}\frac{\partial \bLam_k}{\lambda_{z}}(\frac{\partial \bLam_k}{\partial{\lambda_w}})^{-1}\frac{\partial{\ell}}{\lambda_w}.
\end{align}
Substituting exact derivatives:
\begin{align}
    = \sum^{K+1}_{k=1}\lambda_w\bLam_{z}(\frac{1}{\lambda_w}\bLam_k)^{-1}\frac{\partial{\ell}}{\lambda_w} = \sum^{K+1}_{k=1}\lambda^2_w\bLam_{z}\bLam_k^{-1}\frac{\partial{\ell}}{\lambda_w}.
\end{align}
Replace this with a trace-ratio approximation of the matrix operations to avoid explicit inversion, and map to a scalar:
\begin{align}
    \frac{\partial \ell}{\lambda_{z}} \approx \sum^{K+1}_{k=1}\lambda^2_w(\frac{\tr{\bLam_{z}}}{\tr{\bLam_{k}}})\frac{\partial{\ell}}{\lambda_w}.
\end{align}
This can be straightforwardly adjusted further for parameterization on log-penalties through additional chain-rule calculus, but is omitted for brevity. Without loss of generality, this extends to the flat ridge penalty $\lambda_r$ and all $\lambda_{l,k}$ in place of $\lambda_z$.  

In preliminary simulations and analyses, the trace-ratio seemed to approximate a finite-difference derivative extremely well, and as shown in the performance simulations, yields appreciable performance in practice. However, since this is to the best of our knowledge original work and is not essential to the proposed monomial framework, we omit this here. 

\section*{S.D\quad REML for Correlation Parameters}\label{sec:supp_reml}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{S.E REML for Correlation Parameters}

When a working correlation $\bV = \bV(\bm{\rho})$ is present, $\bm{\rho}$ is
estimated by minimizing a negative restricted maximum likelihood (REML) criterion scaled by $1/N$:
\begin{align*}
  -\log \mathcal{L}_R(\bm{\rho},\sigma^2)
  &= \frac{1}{N}\Bigl[
    -\log|\bV^{-1/2}| + \frac{N}{2}\log\sigma^2 \\
  &\qquad
    + \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}(\by - \tilde{\bmu})^\T\bm{D}\tilde{\bW}^{-1}\bV^{-1}(\by - \tilde{\bmu}) \\
  &\qquad
    + \frac{1}{2}\log|(\sigma^2 \tilde{\bU}\tilde{\bG})^{-1}|^{+}
  \Bigr],
\end{align*}
where $|\cdot|^{+}$ is the generalized determinant, the
product of the nonzero eigenvalues, $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}} = g^{-1}(\bX\btbeta)=\mathbb{E}[\by]$ for link function $g$, $\tilde{\bW} = \begin{pmatrix}
    \ddots & & \\
    & [V(\tilde{\mu}_i)\{g'(\tilde{\mu}_i)\}^2]^{-1} & \\
    & & \ddots
\end{pmatrix}$ is a diagonal matrix of GLM weights, and $\bm{D}$ is a diagonal matrix of user-supplied custom weights. This reduces to classical unbiased REML estimate for Gaussian responses with identity link and no penalization nor constraint. The estimate of dispersion obtained from optimizing this criterion is $\tilde{\sigma}^2 = \frac{\||\bV^{-1/2}\tilde{\bW}^{-1/2}\bm{D}^{1/2}(\by-\tilde{\bm{\mu}}) \|^2}{N-\tr(\bH)}$ which reduces to the usual REML estimate for Gaussian response and identity link, and reduces to a re-scaled method-of-moment estimator for non-Gaussian response. The approximation above is not a typical generalization of REML to non-Gaussian response, but it is convenient for implementation in \pkg{lgspline} since it only requires the moments of the distribution to be known without specifying the full log-likelihood (or quasi-likelihood etc.), and gradients with respect to $\bm{\rho}$ are mostly preserved. 


\section*{S.E\quad GLM Extension: Consistency and Asymptotic Normality}\label{sec:supp_glm}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{S.F GLM Extension: Consistency and Asymptotic Normality}

This section records the informal large-sample motivation for the GLM
extension. This is important, because we do not derive the actual constrained MLE directly, so it is not obvious if properties like consistency or asymptotic normality hold. The construction follows the usual
constrained-projection logic from the Gaussian case and then replaces
the Gaussian precision by the fitted GLM information matrix.

Under standard regularity conditions for a correctly specified penalized
GLM, let $\bhbeta$ denote the unconstrained estimator. Then
\begin{align}
    \bhbeta &\xrightarrow{p} \bbeta^{*}, \label{eq:glm_consist}\\
    \sqrt{N}\,\bG^{-1/2}(\bhbeta - \bbeta^{*}) &\xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\, \bm{\Sigma}), \label{eq:glm_an}
\end{align}
for some target $\bbeta^{*}$ satisfying the imposed equality
constraints $\bA^\T\bbeta^{*} = \bm{0}$, and a positive-definite
covariance matrix $\bm{\Sigma}$. The constrained estimator after a single iteration $\btbeta^{(1)}$ is then
defined by
\begin{equation}
    \btbeta^{(1)}
    = \bU(\bhbeta)\,\bhbeta,
\end{equation}
where $\bU(\cdot)$ is the projection matrix built from the fitted
information matrix. If $\bU(\bhbeta)$ converges in probability to some matrix $\bU^{*}$ (note, as an idempotent matrix, all elements are bounded continuous functions of $\bhbeta$), then Slutsky's theorem gives:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:glm_cmt}
    \bU(\bhbeta)\bhbeta \xrightarrow{p} \bU^{*}\bbeta^{*} = \bbeta^{*}.
\end{equation}
This shows consistency of the ``one-step" constrained estimator. By iterating and improving upon the penalized log-likelihood, consistency of the actual estimator $\btbeta = \bU(\btbeta)\bhbeta$ immediately holds; this is because the penalized log-likelihood after $m>1$ iterations is always greater than or equal to the penalized log-likelihood after a single iteration by construction, (note if $\log \mathcal{L}(\btbeta^{(1)}) \leq \log \mathcal{L}(\btbeta) \leq \log \mathcal{L} (\bbeta^{*})$ and $\frac{1}{N}|\log \mathcal{L}(\btbeta^{(1)})- \log\mathcal{L}(\bbeta^{*})| \xrightarrow{p} 0$ then $\frac{1}{N}|\log \mathcal{L}(\btbeta)- \log\mathcal{L}(\bbeta^{*})| \rightarrow 0$). Under the same
assumptions, for any fixed linear combination $\bm{x}^\T\btbeta$,
Slutsky's theorem then yields an asymptotically normal limit:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:glm_slutsky}
    \sqrt{N}\,\bigl\{\bm{x}^\T(\btbeta - \bbeta^{*})\bigr\}
    \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,\, \tau^2),
\end{equation}
for some $\tau^2 > 0$. Because $\rank(\bU)=P-R$, the full coefficient
vector remains confined to the $(P-R)$-dimensional null space of 
$\bA^\T$ and is therefore singular as a $P$-variate distribution. Fitted values,
derivatives, integrals, and other linear combinations of coefficients remain identifiable. Identifiably of the posterior can also be maintained by defining it as a projection of a non-degenerate $P$-dimensional distribution for $\bhbeta$ by $\bU$, as shown in the Bayesian description of the main text. \


\section*{S.F\quad Approximation Properties}\label{sec:supp_approx}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{S.G Approximation Properties}

The framework has broad approximation flexibility, and is able to approximate any noisy continuous function with independent Gaussian-distributed error terms. A formal proof is not appropriate for this submission, but the intuition is straightforward and worth explaining. By the multivariate
Weierstrass theorem, any continuous $f^*$ on a compact
$\mathcal{T} \subset \mathbb{R}^q$ can be approximated uniformly by a
polynomial. On a sufficiently fine partition, by Taylor's Theorem,
local quadratic functions can in turn approximate that global
polynomial well over arbitrarily small neighborhoods of the support with maximum radius $r$ as quantified by $L^2$ norm. For any neighborhood $k$, we can define an even smaller neighborhood of radius $<r$ and call this ``partition" $\mathcal{P}_k$, containing all quadratic expansions and several more polynomial terms (e.g. cubic or quartic terms). Thus the monomial basis spans the corresponding local quadratic space in any partition, and we have complete control over where partitions are located, how many, and how fine, and if smoothness constraints can be relaxed. By consistency of penalized MLE estimators under usual regularity conditions (which $\btbeta$ is a special case of), $\btbeta$ under certain assumptions converges to a ``true" $\bbeta^{*}$ such that the local polynomial it implies approximates the global polynomial at least as well as the quadratic Taylor Series expansion in terms of variance (it is the minimum variance unbiased estimate of the ``best" polynomial it spans), and thus equivalently minimizes $L^2$ norm \emph{better} than the local quadratic Taylor Series expansion. All continuous functions thus belong to the space of functions that the proposed framework can approximate, assuming independent Gaussian-distributed errors. The argument extends without loss of generality, to any other distribution for which the constrained estimates $\btbeta$ are consistent. The existence of a single ``true" unique $\bbeta^{*}$ is not strictly necessary but makes a formal proof more involved.

This is not a a finite-sample
performance guarantee, nor as particularly impressive as it may seem at first glance, since it does not determine how many partitions are needed, how much data are required, or which smoothness constraints can
be retained without making the problem overconstrained. It is however, important to note for potential future work. 

\section*{S.G\quad Fixed-Penalty Simulation Results}\label{sec:supp_fixed}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{S.H Fixed-Penalty Simulation Results}

This supplement reports RMSE and wall-clock timing for the simulation
study of main-paper Section~5.1 under a fixed penalty of $\lambda =
10^{-4}$ applied to each method's native penalty structure, rather than
data-adaptive tuning. All other settings (sample sizes, noise levels,
replication count $B = 24$, number of cores) are identical to the tuned
case. No wall-clock cap was imposed; every replication ran to completion.

The qualitative patterns match the tuned results. At $q = 4$ the
truncated polynomial fails in every replication at every sample size
when fit without data-adaptive ridge tuning, reported as NaN below,
reflecting quartic design-matrix entries for $t_2 \in [40\pi, 560\pi]$
reaching magnitudes on the order of $10^{13}$ against which a fixed
ridge of $10^{-4}$ provides no stability. The fixed penalty is also too
weak for \pkg{lgspline} at $q = 1$, which shows degraded RMSE relative
to the tuned result; the \pkg{mgcv} methods are less affected because
REML is a relatively cheap outer loop on top of a per-fit factorization
that already dominates cost. The runtime advantage of \pkg{lgspline} at
$q = 4$ widens substantially under the fixed penalty, from the
$5\times$-$11\times$ reported in the main text under tuning to roughly
$42\times$-$72\times$ here, confirming that the tuned-regime speedups
are a lower bound on the per-fit advantage of the block-diagonal
monomial formulation at $q = 4$.

\section*{S.H\quad Single-Predictor Base R Reference Implementation}
\label{sec:supp_simple}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{S.I Base R Reference Implementation}

This supplement references a compact base \textsf{R} reference
implementation of the constrained monomial spline procedure for a
single predictor with a cubic basis over three partitions, intended as
a readable reference rather than a production routine. The script uses
only base \textsf{R} together with the helper functions
\texttt{matsqrt} and \texttt{matinvsqrt} from \pkg{lgspline} for
symmetric matrix square roots, and it follows the four-step
transformed-OLS projection of main-paper Section~3.1. An extension to logistic regression is also included. Extensions to
multiple predictors, interactions, other GLM responses, and structured
correlation follow the same template but are more verbose; the full
implementation is in the \pkg{lgspline} package. The corresponding
script \texttt{simple\_implementation.R} is distributed with the
supplementary materials.

Figure~\ref{fig:supp_simple} shows the unconstrained and constrained
fits produced by this script on a noisy test signal with $N = 150$
points. The unconstrained fits are independent cubic polynomials on
each of the three partitions with visible jumps and kinks at the knots
$t = \pm 2$; the constrained fit is everywhere twice continuously
differentiable while retaining the local cubic-polynomial
representation within each partition.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{simple_illustration.png}
\caption{Reference-implementation output on a single-predictor test
